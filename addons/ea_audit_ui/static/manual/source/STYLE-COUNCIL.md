# Council question: the writing style of the AuditISA user manual and sales presentation

## The decision
Which writing style should the AuditISA user manual use, given that its readers are people in professional audit
practices? And is the sales presentation's style right for the partners of prospective firms? The owner has approved the
manual's content and wants it built into the application now (a "? Help" button on every screen of every firm's site,
updated with each release). The style chosen now is the style ~19 chapters (about 18,000 words) will be revised to, then
translated into French. Pick one style (A, B or C below, or a better-defined variant), and say what to change in the deck.

## Context
- AuditISA: audit software (Odoo-based) for audit firms in the OHADA zone (Cameroon first). Built on the ISAs, ISQM 1 sign-off
  governance, SYSCOHADA. English and French; many users are francophone and read the English UI as a second language, or use
  the French UI. Each firm has its own server. One real client firm is live; practice spaces exist for demonstrations.
- Readers of the manual: partners, managers, seniors, staff auditors (often juniors), firm administrators, quality
  reviewers. They know auditing; they do not necessarily know software. They will open the manual from inside a screen when
  stuck ("Help on this page"), not read it cover to cover.
- The owner is an audit partner, non-technical about software, who insists on: plain words, no abbreviations (ISA, OHADA,
  SYSCOHADA, IT, VAT, MSP/ASP allowed), own words (methodology from a Big Four firm's material is proprietary: never copy).
- The deck (15 slides) is for partners of prospective client firms: ISA basis, automatic reconciliations, three profiles
  (complex / non-complex / very simple), analytics and ratios, combined risk assessment, substantive papers → findings →
  summary of audit differences, review and sign-off, archive, each firm's own server.

## Current manual style (style A, as written) — real excerpts
> Starting a new engagement. This chapter takes you from an empty screen to an audit file with its tasks: you open the
> engagement, answer the engagement questionnaire, and the tool works out the level of the file and the tasks it needs.
> Who: any member of the firm except a read-only user can open an engagement.
> 1. On the Dashboard, click **+ New engagement**. 2. Choose the **Client**. If it is not in the list, create it first ...
> Good to know: The engagement partner joins the team at once. ...
> There is no "don't know" answer: every question must be answered before you can conclude. If you do not yet know, find
> out from the client first.

> Review notes and sign-off. Open the paper and click **Review** in the header. The tips and full requirements are hidden,
> so you see only the work. ... Clear: closes the note, which then reads Cleared. Only two people may clear a note: its
> author, or a manager or above who did not prepare the paper. Notes are never deleted. Cleared notes stay on the paper as
> part of the audit trail. Good to know: A paper with open review notes cannot be signed by the reviewer.

Characteristics: second person, imperative numbered steps with exact on-screen labels in bold, short sentences, "Who:" line,
"Tip / Good to know / Careful" boxes, a one-line "why" now and then, screenshots, 400–1,200 words per chapter. Little ISA
citation inside the manual (the working papers themselves carry the ISA references).

## Style B — firm methodology register (sample rewrite of the same passage)
> 5. Engagement acceptance and scoping. In accordance with ISA 220 (Revised) and ISQM 1, the engagement partner shall be
> satisfied that appropriate procedures regarding acceptance and continuance have been followed before the engagement is
> created. The engagement team shall complete the engagement profile questionnaire, from which the application determines
> the engagement's complexity classification and the resulting scope of required procedures ...

Characteristics: third person, "shall", ISA paragraph citations throughout, reads like a firm's audit manual, fewer
click-steps, more "why per the standards".

## Style C — hybrid (sample rewrite)
> Starting a new engagement (ISA 220, ISA 300). Open the engagement, answer the profile questionnaire, and AuditISA sets the
> file's level and tasks. 1. Dashboard → **+ New engagement**. 2. Choose the **Client** ... Why it matters: the level
> (very simple, non-complex, complex) sets how much you document, never whether mandatory ISA work is done.

Characteristics: same click-steps as A but terser, audit terms of art used precisely (assertions, performance materiality,
combined risk), each chapter opens with the relevant standards and a one-line "Why it matters" tied to the ISA; no
conversational asides.

## Current deck style — real excerpts
- Cover: "Audit files built on the ISAs, sized to each client" / "From acceptance to archive, for audit firms in the OHADA
  zone. In English and in French."
- "Every working paper starts from the ISA requirement" — bullets: "The Objective states the aim of the standard, with a
  link to the latest IAASB text." "Each task names its ISA paragraph, for example ISA 560 paragraphs 6–7."
- "Upload the client's files; the agreements are done for you" — "Trial balance: balanced, assets equal equity and
  liabilities, every account in a lead schedule." "Fixed asset register agreed to the ledger: cost, additions, disposals
  and depreciation."
- "Documented as deeply as each client requires" (three profiles: full documentation / document by exception / tick list
  and summary).
- "A finding is recorded once and flows to the summary"; "Quality controls a reviewer can rely on"; "Each firm gets its own
  server and keeps its own data"; closing "See AuditISA on an engagement like yours" with a contact placeholder.
Characteristics: short declarative titles, 4 bullets per slide, a real screenshot on most slides, no figures claimed
(no time savings, no client counts), no testimonials yet.

## What is at stake
The manual is the daily reference inside a professional tool sold to audit partners; the wrong register either patronises
experienced auditors or loses juniors and second-language readers. Rewriting 18,000 words (plus French) twice is expensive.
The deck is the first impression for paying firms.
